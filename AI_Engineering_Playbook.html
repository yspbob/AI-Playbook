AI Engineering Operating Model Playbook

The executive version is the Twelve Decisions deck.

Converting AI capability into governed engineering value

A practical AI implementation playbook with a focus on engineering, ROI and operationalisation, targeted at mid-size engineering organisations. Yaroslav Pavolotskyi, version 1.0.

Market data and named tools are dated in place and were last verified in August 2026. As the AI space is moving fast, volatile specifics should be revalidated before adapting this playbook for a specific company. The playbook structure is built around the planes, gates and required decisions, which persist while products provide specific out-of-the-box implementations (§2). The defaults here are recommendations for a regulated, mid-size company: where the text says "every" or "only", equivalent controls that achieve the same outcomes count. The document is a synthesis of current market practice and the author's own experience-based judgement: claims without a source are the judgement, with §8 and §10 the tools for testing them locally. I'd start with the entry rung and gradually scale based on the evidence.

The playbook in 60 seconds

AI adoption has outrun returns. Org-level gains, where measured, run at roughly 10%, not 10x. DX's telemetry across 400 companies shows roughly 8% higher throughput over 15 months1. Only 39% of companies see any bottom-line impact at all (McKinsey)2. Roughly 90% of surveyed technology professionals use AI at work, while about 30% report little or no trust in AI-generated code (DORA 2025). While these studies measure different things, they point the same way. Much of the gap traces to three recurring operating-model problems.

  • Problem 1: Realisation. The gain is smaller than the headline because much of it is absorbed by steering and assuring AI output, while for most teams writing the code wasn't the bottleneck.
  • Problem 2: Economics. Agentic AI makes cost variable and uncapped, while the benefit arrives as freed capacity, which evaporates unless explicitly converted into value.
  • Problem 3: Amplifier effect. AI amplifies whatever operating model it lands on. §10 makes this testable: score the eight dimensions, and the weak ones are where it breaks first.

The proposed response pairs one move with each problem:

  • Realisation Engineer the gain: a shared platform, up-to-date context, automated assurance gates. → §2, §3, §4, §5
  • Cash-conversion Consumption FinOps from day one, with every freed hour having a named plan. → §6, §9, §12
  • Operating model Redesign the operating model before scaling AI across it. → §6, §7, §10

Cross-cutting The harness sits under all three: your code, data and knowledge made usable by AI and kept relevant. When it goes stale, agents keep answering from outdated facts. Progress is measured at every level (§8) rather than asserted.

What the document delivers. The document is structured around three practical tools: a scored readiness profile (§10), a wave plan with gates and kill criteria (§11), and a priced business case (§12). Part I frames the problem and the principles (§1). Part II builds the organisational machinery, covering platform, data and knowledge, interpretation, delivery, governance and people (§2-§7). Part III assesses, runs and prices the result (§8-§12).

Part I: Frame

1. Thesis & Operating Principles

The evidence: mid-2026

I start with the gap between task-level acceleration and organisation-level value.

Across more than 100,000 GitHub developers, three generations of AI coding tools increased coding activity by 180% but releases by only 30%. The researchers attribute this attenuation to production's weak links: AI-generated code must still be reviewed, integrated and released by humans. In a regulated environment, the chain from generated code to realised value runs further, through architecture, security, compliance, change control and adoption. Coding gains therefore cannot be carried directly into the business case. (Demirer, Musolff and Yang, 2026)

Through H1 2026 the bills came due. The visible responses were operating-model moves: spend caps, licence pullbacks and tool consolidation, a usage leaderboard switched off. My read is that the operating model was one of the core issues, and better models alone would not have fixed it. §10's readiness profile is the local test, and the named players, numbers and sources are gathered in Appendix A.

What to promise

The promise is freed capacity, open model and vendor options, and held quality, under cost caps. What I won't promise is a hard ROI figure. And the conversion decision, whether the freed capacity becomes throughput, cost-out, tech-debt paydown or shorter time-to-market, sits with the executive committee, outside engineering (§9, §12). (Value maths: §12).

The seven operating principles, by the lever they pull

These seven are the ones I consider relevant: some are taken from reputable sources, credited in place, and the others are my own experience-based judgement.

Realisation

  • Platform over individuals (the playbook's implementation choice). A platform turns local, fragmented speed-ups into organisational impact, and DORA finds AI's effect on organisational performance is negligible without a good one. Put the investment into the golden path, the default workflows. Licences alone have a poor record.
  • Delivery pipeline and engineering craft matter more than before. Version control, small batches, tests, CI and review discipline become crucial once AI raises the volume of code flowing through.
  • Focus AI on your context with an evergreen harness (the playbook's implementation choice). The harness is the machinery that keeps your code, data and knowledge usable by AI: the rules file (AGENTS.md), evals, retrieval and golden paths. Left stale, it starts feeding agents outdated context. Every harness element needs an owner.

Cash-conversion Consumption

  • Cap the spend, convert the capacity. FinOps-for-AI starts on day one, along with the upfront decision whether freed time becomes cost-out or growth.

Operating model

  • Govern proportionate to risk. Tiered controls sized for the AI-specific risks: prompt injection, improper output handling, excessive agent agency, unbounded consumption (OWASP GenAI Top 10, from the open application-security standards body). (§6).

Cross-cutting

  • Measure realised outcomes vs activity. Focus on cost-per-outcome and delivery metrics. Tokens, seats and raw PR volume stay useful as telemetry but shouldn't turn into the optimisation aim.
  • State a clear stance. It reduces both timid under-use and shadow-use.

The strongest objection. Model capability is compounding fast enough that this machinery can look like over-investment in a temporary problem. Agent harnesses already walk a codebase live, and AGENTS.md is now a cross-tool standard the major coding agents read by default (adoption evidence in §3's sources). Given the pace the AI field is moving at, the concern is genuine. So I focused on the buy options for the fact-gathering and drafting stages, starting them at the entry rung (§4).

I'd argue that a company's private decisions don't sit in a vendor model's training data, and certifying them still takes a company expert.

Also, in regulated industries the required control and audit generally rise with autonomy, and no model release lowers that obligation. Faster generation also makes review capacity and value conversion bind tighter (§5, §9).

More capable agents produce more output, faster, and the operating model (conversion, review and cost control) decides whether the extra capacity turns into value. That said, my case shrinks if vendors ship certified organisational decision memory that auditors accept.

Sources (§1)

Evidence confidence: how I tiered the sources, and the method behind it (read before board use)
  • Tier 1, load-bearing: Measured behaviour, and survey instruments with published, validated methodology. DX's 400-company telemetry, Stanford's payroll series, and the Demirer event study across 100,000+ GitHub developers. All three are observational: they measure what happened at scale, and Demirer's matched design is the closest to causal.   • Selection: measurement prevails, with surveys supporting only what measurement has not reached. AI-specific claims rest on 2025-2026 material; older sources appear only for durable principles, such as the productivity J-curve.
  • Verification: every link was checked and every source read in July and August 2026, and time-sensitive material is quarantined in Appendix A with its own date.
  • Defaults: where a number is my operating rule rather than a measured finding, the text says so and names what recalibrates it. Treat the numbers as a starting point.

Part II: The dimensions in depth (the build)

2. Infrastructure & platform

Realisation, Problem 1 (assurance bottleneck)

Exec takeaway. DORA finds that a quality internal platform acts as the distribution and governance layer that turns individual AI speed-ups into organisational impact. Its finding is stark: where platform quality is low, AI adoption has a negligible effect on organisational performance, and where it is high the effect is strong and positive. The rule of thumb for me is to buy or self-host the commodity planes, build only the golden paths, and treat AI agents as first-class platform citizens.

(Builds Dimension 1 of the §10 matrix).

Scope: regulated default, with the lighter cloud-native option alongside. Products as of July 2026, and volatile.

What "platform" means for AI

The platform offers self-service golden paths that encode good defaults. AI multiplies code volume, tool sprawl and variable spend all at once, which makes governing per developer impractical. The platform is the layer at which agents are made both productive and safe, the same job it already does for engineers. The technology is moving too fast to over-standardise practices, tools and methods: that limits both the gains and the ability to adapt. I'd standardise the rails and leave room to change the practice running on them.

Three classes of agents

I split agents into three risk classes. They differ by where the human sits, an organisational choice, rather than by the technology, which converges across all three.

  • Class 1, inline assist. Autocomplete and chat in the IDE. A human accepts every change.
  • Class 2, supervised agents. A human owns every merge regardless of whether an engineer runs the session or delegates it to an agent. Human-led sessions spend under the engineer's identity and team budget, delegated runs under a bot identity on platform runtime. Block (the fintech behind Square and Cash App) runs both modes: ~95% of its engineers use AI, and humans step in at review (Block). At Goldman Sachs engineers supervise Devin's runs and answer for the results. The programme went from pilot to production across its technology division by August 2026 (Fortune, American Banker).
  • Class 3, autonomous agents. No human stands between the agent's work and production. Review comes after the fact, if at all. As of mid-2026 this class is aspirational for general engineering work, with narrowly scoped auto-merges (dependency updates) the long-standing exception. The gates, provenance requirements and agent controls of the rollout roadmap's Phase 4 (§5, §6, §11) exist for this class.

Agent risk has two axes: who owns the change that gets merged, and what the run can touch while it works. The classes track the first. The second is controlled by the sandbox and by the run's own identity, budget and audit trail, cutting across all classes.

Reference architecture: the AI-augmented engineering platform

The platform decomposes into eight planes.

  • Developer experience (IDP: the internal developer portal & golden paths). Self-service portal where humans and agents request capability (Backstage, Port, Cortex). The service catalogue is agent-readable via MCP: agents use it to find what already exists and build on it.
  • Agentic tools (IDE + CLI). Pair an IDE assistant with a terminal agent for heavy lifting (agent Classes 1-2: Copilot, Cursor, Windsurf, Claude Code, Codex CLI). Unattended runs (delegated Class 2 and all autonomous Class 3 agents) run in a sandboxed, centrally orchestrated runtime.
  • Context (MCP + retrieval). Agents search their working repo just-in-time. The code-search index and the managed retrieval layer are exposed as MCP tools. Substance and freshness are covered in §3.
  • Gateway (routing, budgets, guardrails). Model calls cross one AI gateway by design, which provides routing and fallback, per-team budgets, right-sizing (each call to the smallest model that does the job), caching, audit and guardrails (inline policy enforcement on each call: masking personal data and secrets, prompt-injection screens). The gateway is the control point for cost and risk (§6). It is also a single point of failure, so it runs highly available (both LiteLLM and Portkey document load-balanced multi-instance deployment), with a defined degradation mode and a break-glass route that logs loudly, since direct calls run without the gateway's guardrails.
  • Assurance (tests + evals + CI gates). CI tests assure the product. Evals do the same job for the AI system that produces it. Both ship as golden-path defaults. (Langfuse, Braintrust, Promptfoo).
  • Observability & cost telemetry. Tracing, token and cost attribution, and quality signals (churn and change failure rates (§8), eval scores) feed dashboards and the feedback loop. (Langfuse, Datadog).
  • Model plane (in your tenancy by default). In a regulated environment, models run in your own cloud (Bedrock or Azure OpenAI in-VNet, the Azure VPC equivalent, or self-hosted via vLLM) with hard data boundaries. Lighter setups could call cloud APIs through the gateway.
  • Identity & access (who, and what, is allowed in). Each agent gets its own identity: its permissions, its budget and its audit record. Extend your existing directory (Entra added agent identities in 2025, Okta in 2026), and grant short-lived, scoped access rather than standing keys.

Governance (RBAC, quotas and provenance) and FinOps (budgets and showback: cost reporting before internal billing) wrap every plane, attached at the platform rather than per team (§6). The input is the persona: a pre-wired bundle of identity, golden paths, tools, context, limits and gates.

From the field. In 2025 I ran an AI transformation of business operations at a mid-sized professional services organisation. The firm signed fewer than ten master services agreements a year, plus tens of amendments and statements of work. Legal sat at group level, outside the firm's direct control, so thorough redline cycles ran weeks or months, with leadership doing the convincing on both its own and the client side. Many contention points recurred from earlier contracts. We built a paralegal agent to port those past decisions where the business context aligned, and in some cases it cut the redline cycle by multiples. Its evals ran continuously. We backtested against contracts the firm had already signed: the agent reviewed a document it had never seen (held out of the knowledge base), and its findings were scored against what our and the counterparty's Legal had actually settled. Every knowledge system change re-ran the pack. The gaps never stopped appearing, as new counterparties kept bringing clause patterns the knowledge base hadn't seen. Someone had to catch them and update the knowledge base, permanently. I'd budget for evals as a standing operation, not a project phase. Every new signed document was added to the knowledge base or the eval pack.

Governing the model plane: routing policy and fallbacks

The model plane needs a closer look, because it is the one you have the least control over. The models belong to the vendors, who upgrade, retire and block them on their own schedule. So the plane is designed to run several models, and the practicalities are how calls route between models and what to do when one becomes unavailable.

Model routing policy. A routing table covers three of the four routing axes: sensitivity (which work may leave which boundary), cost (routine work goes to the routine tier: the eval gate measures the costs, and escalation on machine-detectable failure, below, bounds it), and availability (fallback chains). My default is a few dozen deterministic rows rather than a trained router. The rows live in the gateway's configuration file: version-controlled, changed by review and, since a config file is exactly what a pipeline can check, enforced in CI (§4). Sensitivity and budget are compliance boundaries: audit-ready and modified through formal reviews, neither of which a trained router easily allows. The fourth axis, complexity, lives in the task rather than in the call's metadata. A table's row limits where a call may go rather than picking the model. By default a call starts on the cheapest capable model in the row's list and escalates when the machine can detect a failure. An engineer who knows the task is hard starts at the frontier model instead. This within-row choice is the one place a trained router could earn its keep, and Bedrock's prompt routing sells exactly that. Which models fill the rows is a separate, eval-driven discipline outside this playbook's scope.

Not everything in that column is a gateway check: the reviewer's fresh context and the bot's sandbox are set up when the persona provisions the run, before any call is made. The table compiles into whatever your gateway consumes. In LiteLLM's terms, the persona becomes a virtual key with a team budget, the model list a model group with its fallback order, and the guardrail profile a pre-call hook, a check that runs before a request leaves the gateway. The workload and sensitivity keys are passed through as request tags, the labels the router matches rows against.

The gateway lookup. At runtime the gateway dereferences rather than decides: the keys travel with the call. The agent's identity carries the persona and its budget, the repo or content set carries its sensitivity tier, and the calling tool declares its workload (autocomplete, chat, agentic task, batch). The row they match is the whole decision.

Escalation on failure works where a machine can detect the failure: compilation broke, tests went red, the output format is wrong. Those calls re-run on a stronger model automatically. Output that passes the checks but is wrong in substance is work for evals and human review. Eval coverage matters here, since a failure pattern generalised into an eval case catches its whole class rather than a single instance. Stale context, a common cause of substance-wrong output, is tackled in §4 through re-certification.

Fallbacks. I'd plan fallback per tier: an outage swaps the failing model, and everything else keeps routing as normal. The frontier tier fails over to a standby, a second frontier vendor kept for hot swap. The routine tier fails over sideways to an active sibling model (preferably another vendor as a hedge), or promotes its traffic to the frontier tier at frontier prices until it recovers.

A few further controls I'd introduce to complete the plane:

  • Pin model versions and treat vendor upgrades as change events, with the eval suite green before adoption and N-1 routable for at least a week, a starting default sized to how long production takes to surface what the evals missed.
  • Track the share of critical workflows dead if a single provider vanishes, and diversify before it crosses your threshold.
  • Wire the provider kill-switch at the gateway: the ability to disable a model or vendor globally, in minutes.
  • Keep the evals portable between models to hold the fallback cost down. Outcome-based suites are model-agnostic by construction (one suite, N scorecards). Performance is a property of the model-harness pair, not the model: the same model can span a double-digit range across harnesses on Terminal-Bench, so a model swap under your tuned harness is an untested pair until the evals say otherwise.

Build vs buy: what fills each plane

In the design I'd focus on interfaces and clean seams, buy or self-host the commodity planes, and build only what encodes the company's context: the golden paths, rules files, routing policies and portal templates. I'd define exit criteria per plane (data egress, eval portability, provenance export) so that any product can be swapped without re-architecture.

The planes are durable, while the tools are only the current fills. Within months Langfuse was acquired by ClickHouse, Portkey by Palo Alto Networks, Windsurf by Cognition and Promptfoo by OpenAI, but no plane changed. Why this shape. Neither the code nor the data is allowed out: code is the firm's IP, and whether regulated data may transit an external LLM endpoint is the compliance function's ruling rather than an engineering default (GLBA / FTC Safeguards, and the EU AI Act). This architecture assumes the strict answer: data leaves the estate only over private endpoints under enterprise terms, and the strictest work runs on self-hosted models (the sovereign tier, above).

Anti-patterns

Licences without a platform (local wins that often don't compound). Tool sprawl (multiple ways to do the same thing). Ungoverned spend (no gateway, no budgets). Agents running as scripts with no persona. In-house building of commodity planes without a strong business case.

  • Route one pilot team's calls through the gateway with budgets on (the estate-wide cut-over is Phase 1 of the §11 roadmap).
  • Provision one agent persona end to end (supervised, Class 2).
  • Put the sensitivity tier boundary in writing.

3. Data & knowledge: the context/harness

Realisation, Problem 1 (assurance bottleneck)

Exec takeaway. AI output is only as good as the context it can reach, and this section's whole job is that reachability, with §4 building on it to turn what's reachable into binding guidance. Retrieval splits two ways: coding agents search code just-in-time, adding a code-search index when scale demands it (a multi-million-line repo as much as a fragmented estate), while the knowledge behind the code (architecture, design decisions, organisational context) needs a managed, permission-aware retrieval layer. I'd keep five things owned in-house: the rules file, source hygiene, freshness, identity pass-through, the eval gate. The failure mode is silent: the model sounds exactly as fluent on stale context as on fresh.

(Builds Dimension 2 of the §10 matrix).

From the field. Building §2's paralegal agent, the first attempt was the obvious one: feed the system tens of original-and-signed contract pairs and ask it to find the patterns. It failed because the model had no view of the business context, the negotiating position or the company's standing in each deal, so almost zero real-world patterns were spotted. We had to templatise the inputs: documents describing the context and positioning, plus the reconstructed redline rounds for each pair. For this implementation, the source of truth stayed where it was, with the data cleaned and structured for the agent's consumption.

Two sources, each with its own retrieval model

Code: agents retrieve just-in-time, with an index added at scale. Claude Code and Codex navigate repos with search and file reads at runtime, steered by the rules file, and Anthropic notes this bypasses stale indexing. That said, an index earns its place, letting agents find what already exists rather than re-implement it (Cursor, vendor-measured: a ~12.5% accuracy lift on its own benchmark, with production A/B gains concentrated in repos above 1,000 files, and independent published cases are still to come). Buying will often be the viable option: platform-native code search if it spans your estate (e.g. GitHub, GitLab), or self-hosted Sourcegraph (a cross-repo search index your agents call as a tool) when the estate is fragmented across hosts. Curate what goes in it: DORA warns that indexing deprecated projects teaches the model the patterns you are trying to retire. The same goes for abandoned branches, so focus on the repositories and branches that represent how you want the code written. The search finds the code, while the decisions and constraints live in the ADRs, the rules file and the certified guidance. These arrive through the document retrieval layer, and the interpretation layer (§4) serves them as guidance.

Where it misses in practice. In my own agent sessions the misses are routine: the model reads past the governing file, or rebuilds what already exists a directory away. Rebuilding is a coverage gap, and the index above addresses it. Reading past a rules file is an adherence gap, and more instruction in the file only makes it worse. Three defences:

  • Machine-checkable constraints move into §4's CI rules.
  • Rules sit close to the work: a directory-level rules file loads exactly where it applies, and §4's pre-flight check makes consulting the guidance a step rather than a hope.
  • §5's independent reviewer agent then checks the output against the same rules after generation, with repeat offences feeding the eval pack.

What slips through all three needs to be caught during the human review on merge (Class 2).

Documents and tickets: a managed, permission-aware retrieval layer. Wikis, ADRs (architecture decision records) and tickets live behind connectors and ACLs (access-control lists), and, unlike code, they carry personal data: names on tickets and edits, people tied to decisions and disputes. Data-protection law is pervasive. The privacy assessment of each source belongs to your compliance function; what engineering owes them is the mechanics: retention set, and a data subject request rehearsed end to end, finding, producing and deleting one person's data across sources, chunks, vectors and traces. I'd have the retrieval layer as a managed service running in your tenancy, through Bedrock Managed Knowledge Base or Azure AI Search. The index refreshes on a sync cadence, so it suits knowledge that stays valid longer than the gap between syncs. Anything that changes faster (a deal's status, a live ticket queue) I'd serve with a live MCP tool call, fresh and permission-checked at the source.

A SaaS layer (like Glean) is a deliberate choice: the docs may already live in your cloud wiki, but that decision was made earlier, and the question is whether a new data class crosses the barrier. For financial services (FS), cross-silo aggregation must prove need-to-know separation, so in practice each silo keeps its own index in your tenancy rather than sharing a firm-wide one.

Owning the harness

The diagram carries the detail of the split. The harness goes stale in two ways: the service's copy that agents actually query can lag your sources, and the sources themselves can lag reality. The latter is why every served item carries two dates: last_verified, when a person last attested it is still true, and valid_until, the expiry that renewal moves, whether by re-attestation or §4's auto-extension. All of it rests on source hygiene: sources recorded, current and curated enough to be worth serving.

Identity pass-through needs deliberate configuration and testing: how well connectors carry source permissions varies, and a misconfiguration leaves agents reading as the wrong identity, anywhere from another user's rights up to superuser scope. While enforcement is the product's job, the verification stays in-house: search the retrieval layer as two differently entitled users and compare the results against what each should see, re-running the check on a schedule as well as on every connector change, and logging it for audit (§6).

The rules file (AGENTS.md), written by hand. It sits at the repo root: project overview, exact build and test commands, style rules that differ from defaults, security notes, PR conventions. Larger repos add directory-level files below it, the nearest one governing its subtree, which is how rules sit close to the work. Version-controlled, it is the cross-tool standard (Agentic AI Foundation under the Linux Foundation, 60k+ repos). Keep it lean: its value sits in pinning the non-standard practices an agent would not otherwise infer.

Freshness as a standing operation

I consider three freshness components: bought machinery, authored judgement, and one-off wiring per source system.

  • Bought: connector re-sync ships with the retrieval service, and the eval runner is an off-the-shelf product (Promptfoo, Langfuse, Braintrust). The eval gate is a step in the CI pipeline: it calls the runner to score retrieval quality and blocks the merge when the score regresses. The runners are self-hosted, because the traces they store contain your code.
  • Authored: the eval content, the questions and expected answers in-house domain experts write, which the runner scores against.
  • Wired: the custom code connecting the tools you already own. Its core is a certification job: it runs daily, refreshes the machine stamps (sync and derivation), moves valid_until on §4's auto-extensions, records expert attestations as last_verified, and enforces §4's expiry rules.

Two gaps remain: schema drift (sources change shape) and ownership churn (owners move on). §4's regeneration loop catches both for anything its estate scan ingests. Content it cannot see (manual processes, external context) drifts invisibly: no machinery can detect a change nobody recorded, so the duty to flag it for re-certification sits with the process owner, and the expiry date bounds how long a silent drift can last. For critical processes, shorten the expiry.

  • Decide on the code-search index using the triggers above: repo scale, estate fragmentation, agents rebuilding what already exists.
  • Pilot AGENTS.md on your busiest repos and compare agent output before and after.
  • Wire the certification job and eval gate.
  • Name the harness owner.

4. The interpretation layer

Realisation, Problem 1 Operating model, Problem 3

Exec takeaway. The gap this layer closes is authority rather than model capability. A frontier model interprets documents well, but it cannot know which of them is current truth, and nothing it concludes is binding. So even the best agents rebuild what exists and extend what should be fenced off, because the governing decisions were never served to them. §3 holds the knowledge, keeps it current and reachable. This layer turns it into the context a specific change needs, and makes it binding. It surfaces the decisions the code implies but nobody wrote down and puts them to a human, certifies which version is true, serves what governs the task at the moment of action, and compiles into CI what a machine can check. The ingredients are proven practice: rules files and ADRs, delivered through the retrieval layer, and structural rules in CI. The playbook assembles them into a full loop adding three conventions of its own: the policy block, the decision ledger and the pre-flight check. That assembly is the playbook's proposal, not yet proven in production.

(Builds Dimension 2 of the §10 matrix to its top level, the harness as a living, drift-monitored product).

Right-sizing. I'd scale this layer to the organisation's demand for it. A smaller estate with fewer load-bearing decisions needs a fraction of what is described here, and the entry rung (below) is where most should stop until the evidence justifies more. This layer formalises the work typically done by your most knowledgeable engineers, a growing rules file and CI fences. That holds until agent volume outgrows the humans behind it, or until you would rather spend that senior attention on strategic work than on review.

Start today, at any scale

The interpretation layer is the certified knowledge about your estate (what exists, what is allowed, what isn't and why), served to agents before they act and enforced by gates after. The name carries the lawyer's meaning, which is an authoritative reading of the sources that shapes what agents produce. DORA's AI-accessible internal data capability covers the ground this layer stands on, and one of its examples gets close to the idea: a review agent that quotes the reasoning behind an old pull request. This layer is built around exactly that slice: the decisions nobody wrote down, and how to capture, certify and keep them current. Some form of it is needed wherever agents run, and what follows is the shape I recommend. The sooner it starts, the less retrofitting there is to pay for.

The entry rung of the layer is:

  • the rules file,
  • a living ADR set with policy blocks,
  • structural rules in CI (dependency and boundary rules enforced by static-analysis tooling on every merge).

It keeps architecture and design consistent while agents multiply the output. That said, manual maintenance stops scaling: once the ADR set stops keeping up with the pace of change, the layer has to be generated, certified and enforced.

The ADR set is the core of the framework. ADRs carry intent, ownership and a review ritual. Each ADR contains a policy block: statements unambiguous enough for an LLM to act on (e.g. "services MUST use config-service", or an allowed-technology list). New ADRs carry the block by design, and for the retrofit an LLM drafts the blocks with a human rectifying each.

Not every convention becomes an ADR: pattern claims, observed regularities in the estate ("tests sit next to the code they cover"), are certified and served as guidance, promoted only when they need enforcement.

Every block declares its enforcement class, which decides who checks it:

  • ci: structural rules compiled into CI checks, run on every merge.
  • query: checked nightly against the fact graph (the map of dependencies, ownership and topology that the pipeline below assembles).
  • review: judgement rules no tool can execute ("a new framework needs a stronger reason than preference"), surfaced to the reviewer through the review context.

Violations of any class land in the conformance register, each assigned to the team owning the violating code. The register is rebuilt nightly, checking every certified rule against the whole estate. CI blocks new violations at merge, so the sweep is what catches the rest: code that predates the rule, merges that used the override path, and breaches of the rules only the nightly query run can check (cross-repo invariants, and rules that break without any merge, like ownership lapsing).

Where the generator (the pipeline's LLM drafting job, below) finds load-bearing structure in the fact graph with no governing decision, it drafts a retro-ADR. Say eleven services send customer notifications through a module no ADR mentions. The generator proposes: "we appear to have decided that all notifications go through X. True?" If the answer is yes, that becomes an enforceable decision. If not, it is registered as an accident: X is fenced off from extension, new notification work goes through the sanctioned interface, and CI enforces both.

For the generation half, the nearest published precedent is Meta, which used agents to read its data pipelines and write context files for them, capturing knowledge that until then lived only in engineers' heads. For the certify-and-enforce half it is CODEOWNERS. The new step is the inference: proposing the decision the structure implies and putting it to a human.

From the field. §2's paralegal agent hit this exact problem outside engineering. Its eval pack needed the decision history for a sample of ten signed MSAs and some fifteen SoWs: what was conceded, what was refused, and the business context behind each call. Almost none of it had been recorded at the time. It lived in mailboxes and in people's memory. Reconstruction took roughly eight person-weeks and involved people from across the organisation, including highly unavailable members of the leadership team. It was a crucial element of the implementation, since the agent's findings could not be scored without it. Capturing decisions at the moment they were made would have been far cheaper.

Expiry follows severity. This is how I'd run it: every policy block carries a severity field, and it decides what happens when an ADR reaches its expiry date. Critical and High ADRs get a grace window for human review (a week for Critical, two to four for High), filled with nudges and escalations. When the window closes with no review, CI blocks new changes in the ADR's scope until it happens. The stale rule stays enforced throughout: an outdated boundary annoys with exceptions, while an open one lets anything through. Urgent work like a production fix can override the block, and every override auto-escalates to the architecture forum or the platform owner. The operational view is a staleness dashboard: every certified artefact (ADRs, context documents, knowledge base entries) with its owner and re-certification date, overdue items flagged.

Sizing. The full loop is a programme: a dedicated build to v1, followed by standing ownership from the enabling team (priced in §12). I'd scope the first loop to the areas teams actively work in. Keeping it certified. Certified knowledge needs upkeep, because every attested item carries an expiry date. Most renewals are automatic: the nightly run rebuilds the fact graph and re-derives the generated knowledge (map pages and pattern claims), so an item found unchanged rolls forward, and owners re-approve only what moved. Automatic renewal applies to estate-derived knowledge only. ADRs are never regenerated, and knowledge carrying business intent (the why and the constraints whose truth lives outside the code) is re-attested by its owner whatever the diff: the nightly run checks the estate against these and routes contradictions to their owners. The renewal class is set at certification, when the owner marks whether an item may auto-extend.

Lapsed knowledge is withdrawn from agents only: people can still find it, flagged "unverified since", and stale rules stay enforced per the expiry policy above. Certified coverage is tracked per content set, and below a floor (e.g. 70%) the harness owner is paged and the set is re-certified as a whole.

Stages 2-3 and the certification load, specified

Stage 2, Draft

Inputs. A domain-scoped fact-graph slice with its coverage metadata, the knowledge already certified for that slice, and the estate-wide vocabulary (layer names, conventions).

Output schema. Everything the generator produces is expressed as structured claims, which are then packaged into artefacts: a map page renders structure claims as a description, a retro-ADR draft carries an implied-decision claim. Each claim holds its text, type (structure / pattern / implied decision), evidence references, scope, confidence, and any counter-evidence found. The claims are the master record.

Automated acceptance. The gate runs mechanical checks before any human sees a draft. Every claim must cite evidence that resolves, and a dangling reference rejects the batch. Pattern claims must state their coverage exactly ("47 of 47 services"), while vague quantifiers ("several") are rejected. Everything must be schema-valid. A claim that contradicts certified knowledge becomes a flagged escalation for human review. Soft claims ("probably", "appears") and claims that change when the slice is re-run are marked low-confidence and routed to human review.

Generator evals. A golden set of estate areas your experts genuinely understand, owned and kept current by the harness owner. The suite measures false-claim rate and known-pattern recall, and re-runs on any model or prompt change, with regression blocking the change. The prompt and schema live in version control and are human-maintained.

Stage 3, Certify

Certification checklist. Three questions per claim: does it match its cited evidence, is that evidence representative rather than cherry-picked, and do implied-decision drafts ask rather than assert (the retro-ADR's "we appear to have decided X. True?"), so the generator never mints a decision on its own. A failing claim goes back to the generator with the objection attached.

Certification load. The work is performed in three lanes.

  1. The auto lane: items whose regeneration diff is empty at expiry. They extend automatically, with the extension logged.
  2. The routine lane: changed items, plus items marked never-auto-extend at certification. Regenerated claims are re-checked against the checklist's first two questions (the third exists only for new implied-decision drafts, which take the decision lane), and business-intent items are re-attested by their owner directly.
  3. The decision lane: retro-ADRs and challenges. Governance work, routed through the existing architecture forum with a timebox and a conservative fallback: an item still undecided after the timebox is auto-fenced.

When a diff is not empty, one of two things has happened. Either the code has drifted from the decision, which becomes a conformance finding to fix or enforce, or the decision itself has changed, and the owner re-certifies it. The attestation load acts as a gate: when it exceeds owner capacity, the served set shrinks, so attestation never lapses unnoticed.

  • Then climb by wave (§11): certify the map pages for active areas, back-fill the retro-ADRs, and add the pre-flight check when agents scale.
  • Scale on evidence: the first loop publishes its certification load, false-claim rate, override rate and conformance outcomes before the second.

5. Process & delivery

Realisation, Problem 1 (assurance bottleneck)

Exec takeaway. AI multiplies change volume, and a delivery system that cannot absorb the increase converts it into instability. As per DORA's most recent annual survey (2025) "AI adoption does continue to have a negative relationship with software delivery stability." The solution is to rebuild the delivery system around the new volume: batches shrink under hard caps, review splits into machine and human tiers, structural changes get their plans reviewed before implementation, and tests and provenance become merge conditions.

(Builds Dimension 5 of the §10 matrix).

Smaller batches

DORA puts small batches and strong version control among the seven capabilities deciding whether AI pays (mapped in §10). It defines "small" as work completable in hours to a couple of days, merging to trunk at least daily. Reviewers request changes on 2% of PRs under 50 lines but 9.4% at 1,001-2,000 lines (Graphite), and across 6.1M PRs batch size is the strongest velocity driver (LinearB 2025). Meanwhile the batches themselves are growing, with median PR size nearly doubling over the year to June 2026 across 500+ organisations (DX Q2 2026). Merging daily needs somewhere to put unfinished work: feature flags decouple deploying from releasing, and for agent-written changes they double as the kill switch.

My starting defaults: a target under ~200 changed lines and a hard cap of ~400 in CI, with declared exemptions for mechanical bulk (renames, generated code, lockfiles), both numbers tuned against your own review data. When a change has to be split, each piece should still make sense on its own. Anything that cannot shrink ships as a stack: a chain of small, dependent PRs, reviewed one by one. Stacking was internal practice at Meta and its peers for years, and GitHub built stacked PRs into the product in 2026.

Review is the new bottleneck

DORA's 2026 ROI work names the "verification tax" as one of the causes of the productivity dip: the time saved on writing moves into assurance. Demirer, Musolff and Yang put the constraint at this stage: in their data pull requests rose 2.5-fold, and each still needs a human decision to merge. Whether that requires more human capacity depends on how much of the increase AI reviewers can take.

My answer is two-tier review. AI reviewers take the machine tier: cheap compared to reviewer time, consistent, and run first. Humans keep the judgement tier: architecture, the system's invariants and blast radius. AI pre-review never replaces approval on risk-tiered paths, and high-blast-radius changes require senior sign-off. Blast radius is a lookup: components carry pre-declared risk tiers, §4's fact graph supplies the fan-in, and a change inherits the highest tier it touches. One rule of my own for the machine tier: the reviewing agent must be independent of the authoring agent, with fresh context, no shared session, and a different model where practical. Models rate their own output too kindly, and shared context shares blind spots, so the value comes from independence: a blank-context reviewer catches what the author cannot see.

Armin Ronacher (creator of Flask), with "probably north of 90%" of a production infrastructure component AI-written: "I still review every line, shape the architecture, and carry the responsibility for how it runs in production." Simon Willison (co-creator of Django) draws the same line: reviewed, tested and understood is professional work, and unreviewed is vibe coding. Charity Majors (CTO, Honeycomb): "AI demands more engineering discipline. Not less."

For changes that create new components, cross solution boundaries, or alter the architecture or design, add a plan gate: the implementation plan is reviewed as well as the finished code, the same move the industry now calls spec-driven development. The agent's plan, carrying its report from §4's pre-flight check if you run the loop, or a short note from the tech lead on what already exists if you don't, names the components it reuses and where the new code lives, and clears review before implementation begins. A plan review is cheap compared to unwinding a parallel implementation after the fact, with the structural rules in CI catching whatever slips through.

Provenance: tag what AI wrote

To govern AI-written code you first need to identify it. The convention is the Assisted-by: git trailer, a machine-readable footer line on each commit: the Linux kernel, Fedora, LLVM and OpenTelemetry recommend it, and Apache uses Generated-by:. A named human still signs off and owns the merge. The trailer is free, greppable and CI-enforceable, it feeds the §8 dashboards, and when something ships broken it is the triage record: which recent changes were agent-authored, by which model, reviewed by whom. It is self-report, so corroboration is part of the design: gateway telemetry shows where agents were active, and CI challenges any merge showing activity without a trailer. Where agents run from stored prompts, version those alongside the code: DORA finds about a fifth of respondents already doing it, and it is what makes an agent's run reconstructable after the fact.

Tests: coverage proves less than ever

LLM-generated tests can execute code paths without meaningfully checking behaviour. Coverage was always gameable, and AI has made it simpler. I'd treat coverage as telemetry rather than a target: all it proves is that the code executed, and whether the behaviour was checked is a separate question. Where failure is expensive, gate merges on mutation score instead: a tool plants small bugs and measures how many the tests catch (Pitest for JVM, Stryker for JS/TS/C#), so it checks that tests notice breakage rather than merely run the code. Meta runs this in production: its ACH system plants domain-specific faults and generates the tests that catch them (Meta, Sep 2025, directional).

From the field. Even in the pre-AI era, the delivery fundamentals were where the speed was. On a tier-1 investment bank's xVA risk analytics platform, delivery speed was constrained by the feedback cycle rather than by how fast code got written. Regression testing was manual, builds were unstable, and intermittent failures, version mismatches among them, consumed the team's attention. We automated the regression pack and the build and deployment paths. The build came down to reliably under 20 minutes, and the standalone integration testing phase mostly dissolved into the pipeline.

We were building gate-based merging before AI raised the volume. In a 2020 transformation of the same platform's delivery, the target state automated every machine-verifiable acceptance check: build, unit and DB tests, system tests and story/epic status checks. The risk-tiered rule above still held. Getting there meant paying for verification capacity explicitly, and expanding dev-in-test headcount, to be revised down as automation matured. Two rules were essential: requirements split into small chunks to avoid large pull requests at the end of a cycle, and the efficiency team funded and protected as a business stream. The AI-era version of this section asks for the same, but at higher volume.

First moves.

  • Adopt the Assisted-by: trailer. Once §2's gateway telemetry is flowing, add the CI check that challenges a merge showing agent activity but carrying no trailer.
  • Set the PR-size target and cap in CI, and pilot stacked PRs on one team.
  • Declare component risk tiers: an LLM-drafted list of critical paths, confirmed by their owners, is enough to start, with §4's fact graph refining the fan-in later.
  • Stand up the machine review tier with an independent reviewer agent, and require senior sign-off on changes touching the top tier.
  • Add the plan gate for structural changes, and mutation score where failure is expensive.
  • Before scaling, baseline the DORA five: deployment frequency, lead time, change failure rate, recovery time and rework rate (§8).

Phase 0-1, §11.

What to watch. Working: throughput rising while change failure rate stays flat or falls. Failing: both rising together, meaning the gates are undersized, so stop scaling and fix the floor (§10).

6. Governance, security, risk & controls

Operating model Consumption, Problems 2 & 3

Most of this section is standard operational discipline applied to a new surface.

Exec takeaway. Whatever the disputed causes of 2026's reported failures (§1, Appendix A), the responses were controls: Uber capped agent spend, Microsoft revoked its own developers' Claude Code licences. No model release supplies those controls. Three control domains cover it, all enforced in the platform (§2): security on agents, model and vendor risk (including concentration), and cost. Any vendor-hosted model is third-party risk, interruptible by vendor or regulator, and frontier models are the least substitutable: the fallback plan and vendor-risk controls exist for exactly that day (Fable 5, June 2026).

(Builds Dimension 6 of the §10 matrix).

Security: agents are a new class of insider

OWASP's GenAI security standards (the LLM Top 10 and its agentic counterpart) each list ten risks. This section takes the four that concern agent conduct: prompt injection, improper output handling, excessive agency and unbounded consumption. The rest are data-side and supply-side risks, with their controls in §3's retrieval boundary, §4's certification and the vendor-risk discipline below. Three incidents set the stakes. Replit's agent deleted a production database during a code freeze, then misreported the rollback. The TrapDoor campaign hid instructions in AI rules files (CLAUDE.md, .cursorrules) shipped inside packages on npm, PyPI and Crates.io, turning the assistant that read them into the exfiltration channel. The Salesloft Drift breach turned one chatbot integration into data theft from more than 700 organisations, the figure in FINRA's alert to member firms. Replit's is the agency case, met by the runtime controls below. The other two teach the supply-chain lesson: extensions, packages, models and MCP servers clear the same bar as any other dependency, with provenance and publisher review, vulnerability scanning, and versions pinned.

The controls are §2's runtime axis written out, and they are classic insider discipline: per-agent identity with least-privilege scopes, approved tools only, sandboxed execution (the agent works on a disposable copy, and its changes reach the estate only through the gated merge), human checkpoints on irreversible actions, logged prompts and actions, and a per-agent kill-switch. FINRA's 2026 agent guidance (Observations on AI Agents) names the matching risks: agents acting beyond intended scope, and autonomy without human validation. The full set applies to supervised Class 2 work from day one and is the entry condition for any autonomous Class 3 run (§11, Phase 4).

Model and vendor risk: validation, and the off-switch

Banks already run formal model risk regimes, under rules requiring every model a firm relies on to be inventoried, governed and independently validated. The UK's PRA is applying its model-risk rulebook (SS1/23) to AI, and US supervisors apply the same discipline through the SR 11-7 lineage. For non-deterministic systems, validation means the eval machinery of §2 and §3: suites re-run on any model, prompt or context change, blocking it on regression. Drift and variance alerts reach the model owner, and injection-test failures block release. All this is done through the assurance plane, so I wouldn't build any heavy additional processes on top.

Fable 5 supplied the vendor lesson. After a published safeguard-bypass report, a US export-control order took the model offline globally within hours on 12 June 2026. The order restricted access by foreign nationals. Nationality could not be verified in real time, so Anthropic suspended access for everyone, and it did not return until 1 July, 19 days later (Anthropic's own account). None of this was specific to Anthropic. Any hosted model can be switched off over your head, by the vendor or by a regulator, and the closer it sits to the frontier, the fewer real substitutes you have. I'd treat that access as a critical third party, using the mechanics that are already §2's model-plane framework: the routing policy, the fallback plan, version pinning, the kill-switch. Deeper continuity planning around exit, audit rights and concentration belongs to your existing third-party-risk framework. This playbook stops at making the fallback technically real. Regulatory details move fast and the local compliance team owns them, so they aren't covered here.

Cost: FinOps-for-AI from day one

Nearly every FinOps team now manages AI spend in some form (98%, up from 31% two years earlier), yet granular monitoring of it is their most-requested tool (State of FinOps 2026). I'd run the controls at the gateway: per-team and per-agent budgets with token quotas, right-sizing with automatic routing to cheaper models, caching and anomaly alerts. Showback first, chargeback once the numbers are trusted. Behaviour-based circuit breakers freeze runaway agent sessions, since the classic blowout is a retry loop rather than human use. And a cap with an exception path is the reference design Uber's own blowout produced: $1,500 per employee per month per tool, exceedable with permission.

Cost accounting uses the token as the raw meter, but the token is not a settled unit: tokenisers differ by vendor, and input, output and cached tokens carry different prices. The common rules are being written by the Tokenomics Foundation (Linux Foundation, launched August 2026, with 30 founding members including IBM, Oracle, SAP, ServiceNow and JPMorganChase), supporting FOCUS, the open billing-data specification cloud providers already use. Until then I'd bill teams in money rather than tokens: every token has a price, but the price differs per model, so money is the only unit that compares across them. And watch the contract as well as the meter: dashboards won't warn you when the pricing model itself changes (§12). The same goes for data terms. In April 2026 GitHub started training on Copilot user data by default for its individual tiers, while the enterprise tiers kept their no-train terms (The Register).

Shadow AI is the counterpart risk. Unapproved use is widespread and bans rarely work. A clear stance and a golden path are the workable alternative (§7, §2).

From the field. At one organisation I know well, AI usage was explicitly banned at group level. Every single person I spoke to used it anyway, to one extent or another. The ban achieved universal usage without visibility, control or telemetry.

First moves.

  • Give each agent its own identity, scopes and kill-switch.
  • Assemble the minimal set of validation evals for your primary model and its standby.
  • Write incident runbooks for bad code shipped, runaway spend, leaked context and losing the primary model.
  • Route one pilot team's model calls through the gateway with budgets and automatic routing to cheaper models (estate-wide is Phase 1, §11).
  • Forecast your renewal under usage-based pricing: the gateway telemetry gives you the numbers.

Phase 0-1, §11.

What to watch. Working: north of 95% of model calls through the gateway, spend variance inside forecast, every incident traceable to a specific agent. Failing: token bills discovered at month-end, agents borrowing human credentials, unapproved endpoints turning up in the network logs. Count the gateway share from network egress to known model endpoints, so the bypassing calls are included, and treat every bypass as either a named exception with an owner and an expiry, like §2's break-glass route, or a finding.

7. Org, people & culture

Operating model, Problem 3 (the amplifier)

Exec takeaway. In McKinsey's data, workflow redesign is among the strongest contributors to bottom-line impact, and licence count isn't among the factors at all. Structured enablement moves usage, and across 135,000 developers DX put it plainly: "simply handing out licenses is not enough." This section builds the three systems behind that: ownership (a funded enabling team), fluency (training for supervision) and stance (clear rules).

(Builds Dimensions 3, 4 and 7 of the §10 matrix).

Ownership: a persistent enabling team

Capabilities, pricing and patterns churn quarterly, so I'd treat the enabling team as permanent. Team Topologies (the de facto standard on team design) frames enabling engagements as short-lived, but that assumes the capability settles, and this one is actively evolving. Developer-productivity teams run at roughly 2-6% of engineering headcount across the 39 companies DX benchmarked (DX benchmarks), which for a 150-engineer org means three to nine people covering enablement, the harness (§3) and golden paths (§2). Booking.com's targeted enablement programme, two-day workshops with office hours behind them, drove a 65% increase in AI adoption, and nearly all its engineers now use the tools (DX case study). That is adoption evidence, while the value test stays with §8. The Dimension 3 test asks for a named owner, a funded programme and accountability for realised outcomes rather than for usage.

From the field. Enabling teams only survive if the capacity is protected and the ramp-up is priced. Back in my xVA risk analytics days (2017), I set up a Platform SME function: named owners per component, up to 50% of their time formally dedicated to the role, and an explicit ramp-up phase of one release cycle in which no delivery output was expected. The success criteria were knowledge artefacts: a populated knowledge matrix, and component documentation kept current with gaps identified, all reviewed by the solution architects. The protection itself was a regular struggle: business priorities would squeeze the 50% down to 10%, and rolling it back took significant effort each time. The AI-era version is §3's ownership and certification machinery, only now the knowledge is served to agents rather than to new joiners.

Fluency: train for supervision, not syntax

Goldman Sachs CIO Marco Argenti frames the skill as describe, delegate, supervise, and warns that "delegating work to an agent without the ability to supervise it is a recipe for disaster" (Goldman Sachs). Supervision means judging work you didn't do. AI belongs in onboarding, where enablement pays back fastest, and JPMorgan already trains every new hire in its asset and wealth management arm on prompts (CIO Dive). I'd rely on champions more than mandates: Uber's write-up of its own rollout, the industry's most candid, found top-down pushes moved adoption less than engineers showing each other real wins (Pragmatic Engineer).

I'd also plan for a seniority inversion: staff-plus engineers save the most time yet adopt least, while juniors adopt most. The junior market itself has shifted. Employment of 22-25-year-old software developers is down about 19% since the ChatGPT launch, on a payroll series Stanford updates monthly (Canaries dashboard, the chart titled "Software Developer Employment Index in Canaries Sample by Age Group"), yet time to a new starter's tenth pull request halves with daily AI use, 91 days to 49 across six enterprises (DX Q4 2025). I'd keep hiring juniors, as agent-natives, and coach them hard. They arrive fluent with agents but without the judgement that makes delegation safe, and it is still built the old way, on reviewed work. Anthropic's own trial shows the stakes: 52 mostly junior engineers learning a new library with AI scored nearly two letter grades lower on comprehension afterwards, worst on debugging, except where they used the AI to ask questions rather than to delegate (Anthropic, Jan 2026). Any one firm can buy seniors instead, but every senior on the market was somebody's junior a decade ago, and bought-in seniors don't know your estate. Coached juniors become your AI-native staff engineers.

Calibrated trust: the culture crux

About 30% of surveyed technology professionals report little or no trust in AI-generated code (DORA 2025). Two-thirds name "almost right, but not quite" as their top frustration (Stack Overflow 2025). Almost-right is the dangerous shape, because it passes the signals a reviewer skims for. Over-trust and under-trust are both calibration problems (Dimension 4). The moves are review discipline as craft pride (§5), honest sharing of failures and no usage leaderboards: Uber's leaderboard helped burn the annual budget in four months (Forbes), and Meta shut down its own as internal costs approached billions (TechCrunch). And §5's provenance trailer is never wired to individual blame, or engineers stop tagging.

From the field. Introducing this operating model at the same organisation as §6's ban story, I saw two reactions dominate. The recognised heroes on critical scope were either defensive, afraid of becoming unnecessary, or simply disbelieving that AI could do their work. And the implementation team itself underestimated the system, treating agents as chatbots with documents uploaded into their context. Facing what a properly designed harness actually takes caused a tangible motivation hit. Both reactions are predictable. I'd name them on day one and keep recalibrating expectations as the harness proves what it can do.

Stance: say what's allowed

DORA finds that a clear, communicated stance amplifies AI's benefits to both individual effectiveness and organisational performance, and that friction, which AI alone does not reduce, starts to fall (DORA capability). The mechanism is psychological: engineers stop asking "am I allowed to do this?" and start asking how to use it. What matters is that engineers actually understand the stance rather than that a document exists. The shape is three buckets (prohibited, permitted with guardrails, encouraged), published, exec-sponsored, living. Shopify's memo is the operational reference: "reflexive AI usage is now a baseline expectation", reach for AI first, by habit. Teams demonstrate why AI can't do a job before requesting headcount. AI use sits in performance reviews. Controls, budgets and provenance can be mandated. Adoption can't be forced the same way, and the memo doesn't try: it sets no usage target, and its teeth are the headcount question and the performance review.

First moves.

  • Publish the stance: prohibited, permitted with guardrails, encouraged.
  • Name and fund the enabling team.
  • Put AI training into onboarding.
  • Open a champions channel with a weekly wins thread.
  • Add trust and understanding questions to the developer survey.

Phase 0-1, §11.

What to watch. Working: weekly-active use rising, review depth holding, trust climbing. Failing: licence counts high but weekly use low, usage rising while trust falls, or shadow tools still growing three months after the stance shipped.

8. Measurement

Cross-cutting

Exec takeaway. Measure what has actually been realised and treat raw activity with suspicion. Expect the J-curve: results dip before they climb, because the spend and the learning land ahead of the gains (§11). DORA's 2026 ROI research names that dip "the tuition cost of transformation", and its warning is that leaders who misread the dip as failure pull funding and lose the return. Instrument before you scale.

(Measurement supplies the evidence column of the §10 scorecard).

If the gain is real, this is where it eventually lands, and every other metric in this section is a leading indicator for these five. The five can tell you that something changed. Whether AI did it is a question for the attribution stack, below.

Attribution runs across everything above. Any series, whether a DORA metric, an AI-layer number or even a survey trend, answers "was that AI?" through the same three-instrument stack (the playbook's own assembly): the provenance split within a team, wave cohorts across teams, and efficiency curves in onboarding time. Mechanics below.

The attribution stack, mechanics
  • Provenance split, within a team. The Assisted-by: trailer sorts a team's merges into two piles, assisted and unassisted, from the same engineers over the same period, since no team hands every task to AI. Whatever hits the whole team (a reorg, a messy migration, new joiners) hits both piles and largely cancels, so the difference between the piles is your best evidence of AI's effect rather than a controlled measurement of it. Two controls I'd apply: compare within a task class (ticket type and size band), because the work engineers hand to AI is never a random sample of the backlog, and hold the definition of done fixed, since METR found the same task comes back with different documentation and test coverage depending on whether AI was available. On each pile I'd watch churn (code reverted, rewritten or deleted within the churn window: 30 days as a default, longer where the release cycle is slower, so the problems AI code surfaces late are caught) and review depth (comments and rounds per change). DX's same-engineer variant, each engineer against their own pre-AI baseline read only in aggregate, is their gold standard. The instrument fades as adoption matures: near-universal use leaves the unassisted pile small and unrepresentative, and the weight shifts to the efficiency curves and your own trend lines.
  • Wave cohorts, across teams. §11 onboards teams in waves, so at any moment some teams have AI and some are still waiting. The waiting teams' numbers show how much everything moves for reasons unrelated to AI (a new CI system, a calmer release calendar), since for them AI changed nothing. Subtract that shared movement from the onboarded teams' gain, and what remains is your best estimate of AI's part: if AI teams rose 15% while waiting teams rose 5%, AI's contribution is nearer 10. The waiting teams aren't perfectly clean, since AI-onboarded neighbours now unblock their dependencies faster, and that spillover means the subtraction leans conservative, understating AI's effect rather than inflating it.
  • Efficiency curves, in onboarding time. I'd take Layer 1's outcome metrics, throughput first among them, and re-index them: each team plotted by month since its own onboarding rather than the calendar, with its pre-onboarding quarter as the baseline. The J-curve becomes a shape you can see, and a new wave tracking below its predecessors at the same stage is an early warning months ahead of its absolute numbers. A dip and then a climb is the shape you budgeted for, and a plateau is fine. Decline after the plateau is the one to act on: I'd check the context is still fresh (§3) and the gates still hold (§5) rather than order more licences.
  • Never read a speed number alone. Change failure and churn belong on the same chart as throughput. A team can always ship more by reviewing less, so throughput up while quality slips is borrowed speed, and the cost returns as next quarter's incidents and rework. DORA tested that trade-off directly and found no evidence that AI adoption softens what instability costs you: it still damages product performance and burnout, enough to negate the throughput gains it appears to buy.
  • Know the limits of precision. This stack is evidence for decisions rather than a controlled experiment. It will tell you whether AI is helping, roughly by how much and where. It will not produce an ROI figure with decimal places, so I'd report direction and range rather than point estimates. For the CFO, one number carries the story: the table's cost per surviving merged change, AI spend divided by merges still in place after the churn window. It should fall quarter on quarter as the wave matures.

From the field. These measurement disciplines existed before AI. AI-scale change volume only raises the stakes. On the xVA platform we ran the same regime from 2021: team velocity along with defect density, technical debt trend and defects reaching UAT and production. Every metric was a trend rather than a point target. The stated rule was a no-blame culture, metrics aimed at the system and never at individuals. We also treated knowledge coverage as one of the essential metrics: SME call-outs (how often the team needed to pull in the subject-matter expert), tracked downward, as the measure of whether knowledge is actually spreading through the team. Tooling was rather typical: Jira, SonarQube, Kibana.

Anti-metrics

Acceptance rate is gameable, and in the agent era it is meaningless anyway, since everything an agent writes counts as accepted. Percent-of-code-written-by-AI is fine as telemetry but makes a disastrous goal. Weighting it by survival, the share that reaches production and stays there, makes it a better diagnostic and no safer as a target: a share of authorship is not a unit of value, and the number moves just as well by attributing more work to AI or by lowering what it takes to ship. Lines of code fare no better: a line written and discarded a week later counts exactly the same as one that ships, so the count measures waste alongside work. Tool DAU (daily active users) tracks habit formation and stops there. Goodhart's law applies, "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure": any usage measure made a target will be hit by the cheapest behaviour. The numbers stay honest as telemetry, because it is the targeting that corrupts them.

First moves.

  • Capture the baseline before scaling: the DORA five, time allocation (where the engineering week actually goes, the baseline §9's ledger needs) and spend.
  • Stand up one dashboard per team, with utilization, impact and cost side by side.
  • Turn provenance on by default.
  • Schedule the quarterly survey.
  • Agree with the CFO in advance how deep and how long the J-curve dip is allowed to be.

Phase 0-1, §11.

What to watch. Working: gains visible in system data on the timetable agreed with the CFO, change failure rate stable. Failing: only self-reported hours improving, or dashboards full of usage while §9's ledger stays empty.

9. Value capture

Cash-conversion, Problem 2

Exec takeaway. Freed capacity requires an explicit decision on where it goes, made in advance of the rollout and reviewed regularly. Capacity does not convert itself: Bain finds coding assistants typically yield 10-15% productivity gains, and that "often the time saved isn't redirected toward higher-value work" (Bain 2025). Pair generative AI with end-to-end process transformation and Bain's range moves to 25-30%. The figures are assistant-era. Agents free more time, and the question of where it goes only gets bigger. McKinsey makes the same point at scale, across all business functions: by the end of 2025 almost nine in ten companies had deployed AI in at least one of them, while 94% reported no significant value from it (McKinsey, Apr 2026).

(The economic half of Dimension 3, and the §10 test is "value-capture decided deliberately").

Right-sizing. I'd scale this machinery to the value in play. An organisation booking a few hundred thousand a year in freed capacity needs a fraction of what is described here, and the ledger alone is where most should stop until the numbers justify more.

The four conversion routes

  • Throughput: shipping more roadmap. It counts only where there is demand, so the freed capacity is aligned to the value streams that have it. The cleanest published demonstration comes from consumer app marketplaces rather than enterprise delivery, but the mechanism is the same: Demirer, Musolff and Yang found more new apps arriving with no rise in total usage (2026).
  • Cost-out: the easiest and most obvious lever is external headcount (contractors and vendors) before your own people. JPMorgan's investor-day language ("resist headcount growth where possible", CFO Jeremy Barnum, Banking Dive) is the pattern rather than layoffs-first, and in the UK and EU cost-out triggers consultation duties (works councils, collective redundancy rules), so route this through HR and legal before any announcement.
  • Quality and tech debt: the route Morgan Stanley reports. Its legacy-code tooling processed nine million lines and saved an estimated 280,000 developer hours in 2025, framed as debt paydown (Morgan Stanley via WSJ, covered by Entrepreneur, self-reported and unaudited).
  • Time-to-market: often the fastest client-facing lever, with vendor-reported compressions like a feature cycle falling from 24 days to 5 (Rakuten case study, directional).

Executing the conversion belongs to ordinary portfolio and workforce management rather than to this playbook. What it adds is the input for those processes: a measured, attributed capacity number (§8) and an explicit route decision, recorded in the ledger below.

The public record is the warning: Klarna (Forbes) and Amazon (Axios) both made loud AI headcount claims and later softened or reversed them. Salesforce is the sharper case: it made the cuts, 4,000 support roles handed to its own agents (Fortune), yet it still hires engineers. Benioff's own read: even the top AI companies keep hiring them, a sign "the models are not at that level yet" (ITPro). While agents may yet make some of those claims come true, that doesn't change the lesson: first prove the capability, then plan the headcount, and announcements come last. The positive reports worth studying are infrastructure stories rather than headcount stories: Stripe describes unattended agents turning tickets into pull requests on a 30-million-line codebase, thousands a week with humans still reviewing (Stripe at the Background Agents summit, self-reported, and volume rather than value).

The capacity ledger

I'd keep the capacity ledger itself to one page per quarter. Freed hours are measured per §8's system metrics at team level rather than per developer, and booked to a named route by an explicit portfolio decision. A row is a team-quarter: measured freed hours, the route, the outcome booked against them, and the owner who signed. Whatever goes unbooked registers as lost. DORA found the same thing happening: in some organisations, feeling faster simply raised what was expected of the team, so the gain was absorbed before anyone could book it. Book outcomes rather than intentions: shipped roadmap items, removed cost, debt burned down, cycle time cut. For services companies the test is harsher, because freed hours convert only into billed utilisation or removed cost, which makes chargeability the metric.

First moves.

  • Ask the CFO which route they would pay for. Their answer sets the prioritisation.
  • Pick the primary route per team for the next two quarters.
  • Create the one-page ledger (its freed-hours column comes from §8's baseline, so that lands first).
  • Put conversion on the monthly ops review rather than hours saved.

Phase 0-1, §11.

What to watch. Working: the ledger reconciles, booked hours within about a third of measured freed hours, and every booking has an owner (the team's engineering lead proposing, finance signing at the monthly ops review). Failing: the gap widens with no explanation, or teams measure freed hours quarter after quarter and book nothing, the absorption pattern DORA describes above. The numbers are direction and range rather than decimals (§8), and the third is a starting default: tighten it once a few cycles show your actual spread.

10. Readiness model (8 dimensions)

Operating model, Problem 3 (the amplifier)

Exec takeaway. AI amplifies strengths and weaknesses alike (DORA's central 2025 finding), and the weakest dimension is where the delivery system breaks first. Score the eight dimensions and focus on the lowest-scored ones. Then the §11 gates decide when the next teams come on board.

Why readiness decides the outcome

DORA's 2025 survey of nearly 5,000 technology professionals and its capabilities model supply the evidence: "The value of AI is unlocked not by the tools themselves, but by the surrounding technical practices and cultural environment." Carnegie Mellon SEI and Accenture arrive at the same place by a different route: their maturity model scores whether the engineering practices and governance underneath the AI hold, rather than how much of it you have deployed.

The mechanism is the Theory of Constraints, which DORA's report also leans on: every system has a limiting factor that governs what it can deliver, and speeding up anything else just grows the queue in front of it. DORA's example is a team that put AI on code review, its real bottleneck, rather than on writing more code that would only lengthen that queue. More volume does not widen the constraint, so the cost of a weakness grows with adoption, and one bad enough event, whether runaway spend, a breach or an outage that reaches customers, can hand back the accumulated gains. What this playbook adds is the floor: the lowest score marks the dimension it is not yet safe to scale on. The floor says where scaling is likely to break things. The bottleneck is where the flow is stuck today, and the way to find it is value stream mapping, as DORA recommends.

From the field. Raising the delivery floor was a multi-quarter programme itself. On the xVA platform, each improvement stream (regression automation, build stability, deployment automation) carried an estimate of three to nine months with the teams and SMEs we had, and the streams had to be sequenced against delivery commitments. AI compresses some of this, but the prioritisation against business deliverables and the SME time remain the challenge.

The readiness scale (applies to every dimension)

The scale is the playbook's own, with the levels as labels for a capability profile. No level is an end state, and I'd treat L4 as an aspirational bet rather than a destination.

Level Name What it means
L1 Ad-hoc Practice is individual and invisible. No shared standard; results depend on who happens to be doing the work. AI use is personal, ungoverned, unmeasured.
L2 Developing Pockets of good practice and some shared tooling, but inconsistent across teams and largely unmeasured. AI adopted opportunistically.
L3 Defined Standardised, governed and instrumented across teams; the approved path is the one already provisioned. AI use is sanctioned, supported and measured.
L4 Self-improving The measurements feed back: evals, cost and quality signals tune the system on their own cadence.

The gap that matters. I'd expect organisations strong on classic engineering to still sit at L1-L2 on the AI-specific aspects: current context, eval suites, token governance, a communicated stance, because these are new disciplines with no legacy strength to inherit. You will not see the gap unless you score the AI-specific criteria. Target L3 across the board before scaling. Reserve L4 for the one or two dimensions that are your strategic bet.

11. Sequencing & roadmap

Cross-cutting

Exec takeaway. In mid-2025 Gartner predicted over 40% of agentic AI projects would be cancelled by end-2027, naming escalating costs, unclear business value and inadequate risk controls as the causes. McKinsey finds about two-thirds of organisations have not scaled AI enterprise-wide. Each of those causes maps to groundwork this section front-loads: budgets, a value baseline and controls are in place before scaling starts. The path is five gated phases, readiness to autonomous agents, and each gate is passed on evidence rather than on the calendar.

(The lowest §10 dimensions become Phase 0's backlog, and the §10 C-suite gate is Phase 3's entry criterion).

Five phases

The durations below are starting points, and system and organisational complexity stretch them. The exit gates decide when a phase ends.

Phase 0, readiness. Score the §10 matrix with evidence before buying licences. Tooling bought ahead of the groundwork risks spending money amplifying the existing inefficiencies. Exit gate: the lowest dimensions have an owner and a plan.

Phase 1, foundations (about a quarter). Gateway live with budgets from day one. Stance published (§7). Baseline captured (§8). First cut of the harness on one content set (§3). One golden path end to end (§2). The shared infrastructure is built once. Everything else starts small and grows wave by wave. Exit gate: over 95% of calls go through the gateway.

Phase 2, pilot (a quarter, one or two teams). I'd pick the cohort rather than let it self-select: one or two representative teams, trained for supervision by the enabling team (§7) before the pilot starts. Check the prerequisites, too: the classic disciplines in the lower levels of dimensions 1 and 5 of the §10 matrix, things like CI/CD, version-control hygiene and review discipline, need to hold on the code the AI rollout will target. The support matters as much as the licences, and §7's evidence says why: enablement and champions move adoption, licence-dumping doesn't. Choose the pilot's work by scoring candidate workflows on value, frequency, verifiability, blast radius and data sensitivity. Then push for a daily habit, since Booking.com found value concentrates above roughly 12 days of use per month. Exit gate: throughput up with change failure rate flat, spend inside the cap, and no unmanaged incidents. If usage sits below the 60-80% band of the pilot's developers using the tools in a given week (a starting band, adapted from GitHub's month-one activation benchmark), the pilot has not produced enough evidence to judge, and the answer is more enablement rather than a verdict. I'd analyse usage to decide whether the pilot can be read, never whether it passed, which is §8's rule.

Phase 3, waves (two to four quarters; entry: the §10 C-suite gate). Scale by repetition: each wave onboards the next cohort of teams through the sequence the pilot proved, training first, then the golden path, then the daily-habit push. A new cohort needs to satisfy the same engineering prerequisites as the pilot. A cohort with gaps gets the foundational engineering work done first (§10's field note). I'd run one wave per quarter, with enablement travelling alongside and champions from earlier waves carrying the peer proof. I'd expect everyone to hold a licence, and usage to settle into a spread, from engineers in the tools all day to a few sessions a week. So plan the value case on that spread rather than on uniform heavy use. Exit gate per wave: the pilot criteria plus a non-empty capacity ledger (§9).

I'd also plan the waves starting from the top of the organisation's value stream. Adoption changes the team's operating model and flow, which will likely be felt at the seams between teams. A team that adopts AI ahead of its consumers floods them with volumes they can't process, cluttering the downstream team's backlog and potentially turning the capacity question into a political one. The mirror case leaves the adopter starved. If the upstream team works the old way, the gains might be sitting idle waiting on inputs, and the pilot flashes red on the executive dashboard, while the constraint was never within the adopting team. So when choosing teams for a cohort I'd analyse whether any up- or downstream teams might drive the adoption into this pattern, and then decide on the sequencing correspondingly.

From the field. At some point the xVA platform ran a separate DevOps team, and that seam was the first to drown. As the development teams became more efficient at producing code, the release and environment work all converged on that one team. Its queue became the programme's pacing item, and no amount of developer-side speed moved the delivery date. Addressing it took a department-wide automation programme, including repo restructuring and a shift in the DevOps operating model. The pattern predates AI, with AI causing the same challenge many times faster.

Phase 4, autonomous agents on golden paths. This phase covers Class 3 only, the agents of §2 whose output reaches production without a human owning the merge, while Classes 1 and 2 have been live on the golden path since Phase 1. I'd start only after Phase 3 holds, with agent personas provisioned by the platform (§2), §6's agent controls in force and agent metrics live (§8). Exit gate: provenance complete on every agent change (§5), and churn on agent-authored changes at or better than the human baseline (§8).

When a gate is missed

Every missed gate gets one of three dispositions, agreed with the CFO in advance: hold (fix and re-test), shrink (reduce the scope of the wave) or kill (fold the learnings into the stance and stop). Name the gate's owner up front. I'd spend the effort on the owner and the dispositions rather than on further refinement of the numbers.

Managing the J-curve

The shape is borrowed from economics. The productivity J-curve (Brynjolfsson, Rock and Syverson) shows that general-purpose technologies dip before they pay, because the early quarters go on intangibles: process redesign, skills, new workflows, investment that accounting expenses rather than capitalises, so the metrics read it as pure cost. DORA measured the same shape in AI-assisted delivery and named the dip the tuition cost. Its model identifies three drivers: the learning curve (developers are slower while they learn what to delegate), the verification tax (AI produces volume immediately while review capacity scales later) and pipeline adaptation (the §2-§5 foundations, all cost in the early quarters). This playbook adds a fourth: late-surfacing defects, where early code that slipped past immature gates comes back. Spend, meanwhile, is front-loaded, so cost peaks where output dips. That is why the classic failure is pulling funding at the bottom. I'd budget the tuition (§12), pre-agree the review calendar with the CFO and hold the gates through the dip.

From the field. The dip naturally feeds into a stakeholder issue. In our case the budget holders expected AI to replace people quickly. As we travelled towards and through the trough, frustration built: the recurring complaint was the absence of an "agentic model implementation" while we were building the foundations, and the unglamorous work on process and data cleanliness read as overcomplication. The lesson: pre-agree the dip with the budget holders before the spend starts, then keep explaining what is actually happening.

Anti-patterns

Big-bang licence purchases (the spend arrives before the workflows are reshaped, §7). Tool-first rollouts (the value sits in reshaped workflows rather than in the tools themselves). Usage leaderboards (§7). Skipping the baseline, which leaves the gain unprovable. Scaling on self-reported enthusiasm (§8). And waves picked without considering the dependencies between teams and units along the value stream, which sends the adopters' volume into neighbours who can't absorb it, or leaves the adopters starved by neighbours still at the old pace.

Where not to start

Openings I'd avoid: high-blast-radius changes with weak rollback or observability. Codebases with poor tests, unclear ownership or stale docs. Workflows that cannot be cheaply verified. Agentic autonomy (Class 3, §2) before identity, budget, scope and audit are in place (§6). And politically attractive, economically vague cases. Start where saved time converts (§9).

First moves.

  • Collect every section's First moves into the entry plan: each block is mostly that section's slice of Phases 0 and 1, so together they cover it.
  • Write the wave plan with named gates and numbers.
  • Agree the gate dispositions with the CFO before the pilot starts.

What to watch. Working: waves pass their gates without heroics, and the plateau arrives roughly where it was planned. Failing: waves launched on the calendar rather than at the gates, or pilot metrics redefined mid-flight without anyone announcing it.

12. Business case (economics)

Cash-conversion Consumption

Exec takeaway. Build the case bottom-up from your own numbers, promising capacity at governed cost (§1). Year one is negative by design: the foundations arrive before the value. The model prices three things: the capacity created, the share management books through §9's ledger, and the full programme cost. It is illustrative and directional, and every number in the companion workbook is a starting default to replace with yours.

(The case holds only if two things exist: the telemetry to measure the gain (§8) and the ledger to book the value (§9). The swing variable is management behaviour rather than technology (§10).)

The model

Value per year = engineers × fully-loaded cost × capacity freed × conversion
Cost per year  = tooling + enabling team + platform + training + instability
Net            = value − cost, judged cumulatively over three years
  • Capacity freed: the engineer time AI frees after its own overheads, as a share of all engineer time. Learning time and the verification tax show up here as a lower freed share, heaviest in year one, rather than as separate cost lines, so they are never counted twice.
  • Conversion: the share of freed capacity you actually pick up and book as value. Owned by the executive committee rather than any single leader, since booking capacity moves roadmaps, budgets and headcount at once, and the only benchmark that matters is your own §9 ledger.
  • Captured (freed × conversion): the share of payroll the model books as value, and the number to compare with what the market measures below.

Value is priced in loaded engineering cost throughout: a payroll-equivalent measure of the capacity freed and booked, rather than cash, EBITDA or recognised financial benefit. The actual financial impact depends on the route chosen in §9. Removed contractor spend and avoided hires reach the P&L directly. Extra throughput is valued by what the shipped work earns: incremental margin, or revenue brought forward by an earlier release. Redeployed capacity is real but books no saving. The model keeps one denominator as an example because each organisation chooses its own route, and the recognition question is settled with the CFO.

The cost stack, per year:

  • Tooling: seats plus tokens, all-in per developer-month.
  • Enabling team: the §7 team, additional to the engineers in scope.
  • Platform: gateway, observability, retrieval and evals, priced as the AI-specific layer only.
  • Training: cash costs only, facilitation, courseware and compliance.
  • Instability (optional): if shipping faster raises the change failure rate, the extra incidents and recovery time carry a cost. DORA prices it mechanically: deploys × failure-rate rise × recovery hours × cost per hour. Zero until §8's telemetry says otherwise.

From the field. The harness is the cost most often under-priced. One project planned the source-data and context work at roughly a quarter of the effort and it ran closer to 60% once the first serious agent was built: context documents, eval suites, decision-history reconstruction and ongoing maintenance. I'd price the harness as a first-class item from day one.

Worked example: 300 engineers, London

All of it adjustable in the workbook:

  • Engineers: 300 at a £160k blended fully-loaded cost, a £48M payroll in scope.
  • Tooling: £320 per developer-month all-in, charged at 60% average coverage in year one to reflect gradual adoption as the §11 waves land.
  • Enabling team: nine experts (3% of headcount) through the rollout, dropping to six in the steady state.
  • Platform: £72k a year, the AI-specific layer only, with year one at 60%, as with tooling.
  • Training: £510k in year one (£1.7k per engineer: external facilitation, courseware and one-off compliance), £240k after (£0.8k per engineer: refreshers and materials).
  • Instability: zero until §8 says otherwise.
  • Total: £2.68M in year one, £2.42M at run rate. 5.6% of payroll, falling to 5.1%.

Surveyed AI budgets look smaller (DX: nearly half of leaders set aside 1-3% of the engineering budget) as they count only the tool invoice. On that scope our tooling line is 2.4%. The rest of the stack is mostly people cost (the enabling team and training), which is not accounted for in the tools budget.

All three scenarios sit inside the range the market actually measures: DX's observed 5-15% band (§8's calibration table) sets the order of magnitude for organisation-level movement, and this model's outputs stay in that region. Everything below is time-sensitive market evidence, accurate as of July 2026. It supports the durable argument in §1 but will age faster than the playbook does. Revalidate before board or client use.

(The factual story the thesis rests on: named players, real numbers, a recognised problem and emerging solutions).

The surge. By 2026 the industry has committed. The Stack Overflow 2025 Developer Survey finds 84% of developers use or plan to use AI tools, up from 76% in 2024, and the frontier numbers run higher still. Google's Pichai says ~75% of new code is now AI-generated (up from ~25% in 2024), with the workflow "truly agentic": engineers supervising autonomous AI rather than prompting it. Microsoft says 20-30% of its code is AI-generated (Nadella, Apr 2025), and Copilot adoption is widespread across the Fortune 100. At organisation level, the median across 400 companies is ~8% (DX), and Google publishes the code-share figure but never an org-velocity one. Meanwhile developer trust is thin: only 33% trust the accuracy of AI output, with more (46%) actively distrusting it, and 66% are frustrated by code that's "almost right, but not quite". I'd not read these measures as like-for-like: together they set a measurement boundary, and code share cannot stand in for organisation-level throughput or economic value. (Stack Overflow 2025, Fortune/FastCompany)

The reckoning. Then, through the first half of 2026, the bills arrived and the doubt set in. Publicly, and with names attached:

  • Uber burned through its entire 2026 AI-coding budget by April: engineers averaged $150-250 a month with power users at $500-2,000 (Forbes), and it capped spend at $1,500 per employee per month per tool. Its COO openly questioned whether the spend is worth it. (TechCrunch)
  • Microsoft revoked its own developers' Claude Code licences only months after enabling them, as it moved to standardise and govern its AI tooling (cost was one driver). (TechCrunch)
  • Meta shut down its internal AI token-spend leaderboard as internal costs approached billions, and Instagram head Adam Mosseri went public predicting per-engineer token caps within a year or two (foreseen rather than implemented). (TechCrunch)
  • Priceline's routine Cursor renewal came back 4-5x more expensive under usage-based pricing. (TechCrunch)
  • The headlines turned with them: "the token bill comes due" and "the AI adoption spending spree is over, time to focus on value" (CIO).

The bills exploded due to a structural shift. The paradigm moved from autocomplete, which was cheap and bounded, to compute-hungry agents, and pricing moved with it, from predictable flat per-seat licences towards variable, usage-based metering (Cursor shifted to usage-based in 2025, and enterprise agentic tools now bill "seat + actual token usage"). Vendors meter because a single heavy agent-user can cost them thousands a month, which a ~$40 seat cannot absorb. For the buyer, that swapped a predictable, capped cost for a variable, uncapped, volatile one, which caught even Uber and Microsoft out. The counter-intuitive part is that per-token prices are falling while total bills rise, because usage scales faster than prices drop. My read: AI cost is now a function of how you use it, which is a lever under your control rather than a fixed line item. (TechCrunch)

And often the value wasn't landing to match the spend. It's all survey data, but three firms asked separately and got the same answer. McKinsey's State of AI (Nov 2025) finds only 39% of organisations report any enterprise-level EBIT impact from AI. Most of those put it under 5% of EBIT, only ~6% qualify as "high performers", and nearly two-thirds have not yet begun scaling AI across the enterprise. Gartner's April-2026 survey of 782 I&O leaders shows the same picture at project level, with only 28% of AI use cases meeting ROI and 20% failing outright. And Bain's 2026 Pathfinder survey (n=951) found ~40% of companies that tracked it booked under 10% cost savings against a typical target of 11-20%, yet 90% of those underdelivering still plan to raise AI budgets, so budgets keep rising even where the savings have not appeared. (McKinsey 2025, Gartner, Bain)

The diagnosis the leaders now share. VentureBeat's "Agentic Reckoning" calls it a runtime rather than a model problem. McKinsey finds workflow redesign among the factors most correlated with measurable EBIT impact. DORA's follow-up ROI of AI-assisted Software Development report (Apr 2026) gives the value story its shape, a J-curve with a tuition period in which AI slows delivery before strong foundations convert it into gains. The fixes the big players are converging on are, likewise, operating-model fixes: FinOps-for-AI (per-user and per-agent budgets, token quotas, automatic fallback from premium to cheaper models, spend dashboards, chargeback to business units), governance as architecture, and embedding AI into redesigned workflows.

What it suggests. By mid-2026 Google was reporting three-quarters of its new code AI-written with engineer approval, while Uber had capped the same class of tools after a budget blowout. Still, Google runs its own models and Uber buys them, so the economics differ. I'd take the contrast as illustrating the operating-model difference: it names what to test locally, pipeline governance, consumption control and delivered value.
